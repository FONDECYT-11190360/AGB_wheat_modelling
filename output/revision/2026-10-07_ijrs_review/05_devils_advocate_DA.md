### Genuine strengths (brief)
The paper is unusually candid. It reports a baseline that beats most of its own satellite models, uses fixed hyperparameters so the held out field never selects a configuration, reports per fold results (Table S2), computes R² against the held out mean, runs a sensitivity analysis excluding sowing zeros, and states in §4.6 that rankings across four folds are highly uncertain. Several criticisms below target the gap between this candour in the body and the firmer framing in the abstract.

### Strongest Counter-Argument

The paper's headline is that accumulated thermal time "carried most of the AGB signal that transferred between fields." The evidence does not isolate thermal time; it isolates a linear model. Every satellite predictor set was fitted only with random forest and XGBoost, while ∑GDD received the one model family that extrapolates and pools trends. When the comparison is made within the same algorithm, the paper's own Table 5 reverses the story: Sentinel-2 alone (RF 8.08, XGBoost 7.93 t ha⁻¹) transfers as well as or slightly better than ∑GDD alone (RF 8.19, XGBoost 8.28), and RF with all predictors (7.04) beats RF on ∑GDD by about 14%. The authors invoke exactly this same-algorithm logic to argue for a "genuine Sentinel-1 contribution", yet do not apply it to Sentinel-2 or to the All set, where it would show that satellite predictors carry at least as much transferable signal as thermal time. A sceptic would conclude that the paper has shown that trees trained on three fields cannot extrapolate (La Cancha reached 1213 °C d, outside the training range; seven samples above 30 t ha⁻¹ sit in two fields), not that remote sensing adds little.

The same sceptic would note that the design is about 29 sampling dates, not 153 independent samples: ∑GDD is constant within a date, 21 of the 153 values are AGB = 0 assigned at sowing rather than measured, and the ∑GDD baseline uses a base temperature switch tied to field observed phenology. Finally, the one field whose crop condition departed most from the thermal time expectation (Hidango South, low AGB) is exactly where PlanetScope RF (4.91 t ha⁻¹) and All RF (5.43) beat the baseline (6.69). Fold means dominated by single domain shift failures (PlanetScope in the spring wheat field, Sentinel-2 in La Cancha) hide this. The more parsimonious reading is "linear thermal time beats unconstrained trees on four fields", which is a narrower and less novel claim.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths
1. Model form, not predictor content, explains the headline. A linear or monotone model on a single cumulative satellite index may transfer as well as ∑GDD; tree models fail mainly by not extrapolating beyond the training range (La Cancha thermal time and AGB). This is more parsimonious than "thermal time carries the signal" and fits the matched-algorithm rows of Table 5.
2. Tie breaking rather than canopy information explains the S1 same-algorithm gain. Within-date variation of an unfiltered single pixel backscatter series lets trees escape the nearest date step function; the paper acknowledges this but runs no null control.
3. Satellite failures are domain shift, not lack of signal. PlanetScope failed in the spring wheat, 2020 Dove-era field; Sentinel-2 failed in the out-of-range La Cancha field; both did well in Hidango South, the field with an atypical crop condition.
4. The baseline's edge partly comes from field observed phenology (base temperature switch at observed tillering) and from 21 sowing zeros that only season-progress predictors fit trivially.
5. Data anomalies (AGB up to 43.5 t ha⁻¹ from 0.25 m² quadrats) rather than model structure may drive the La Cancha errors and much of the fold variance.

### Missing Stakeholder Perspectives
- Crop modellers and agrometeorologists, for whom thermal time baselines and phenology driven growth curves are standard comparators.
- SAR specialists, given the unfiltered, mixed incidence angle Sentinel-1 series on which the S1 conclusions rest.
- Growers, advisers and insurers who would bear the cost of commercial PlanetScope data relative to free Sentinel and station data.
- Agencies that would need the method where no nearby station or phenology observations exist.

### Unexamined Premise
The paper assumes that predicting point level AGB from 0.25 m² quadrats is the right target for 3 to 60 m satellite pixels. With a within-date scatter of about 3.4 t ha⁻¹ and ∑GDD constant across points, the response is effectively a set of 29 date means plus quadrat noise, so the experiment mainly tests who best reproduces a seasonal trajectory, which is the question thermal time is built to answer.

### Observations (Non-Defects)
- The decision to fit ∑GDD with RF and XGBoost (§2.3.4) is a good control and is what exposes C1; extending it to linear satellite models would complete the design.
- Two-sided Kalman smoothing and cumulative sums from sowing are appropriate for retrospective in-season estimation, and the paper correctly states that real-time use would need a causal filter.
- Held-out permutation importance with grouped permutation is a sound choice given the correlated predictors.
- The negative result, honestly reported, has value for a remote sensing audience regardless of how C1 resolves; the remedy for C1 changes the wording of the main claim more than the paper's usefulness.
