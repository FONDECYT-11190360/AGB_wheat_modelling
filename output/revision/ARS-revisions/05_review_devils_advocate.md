## Strongest Counter-Argument

The paper's central claim is that "most of the transferable information about wheat AGB was captured by thermal time, and satellite predictors provided at most modest and configuration-dependent gains" (Conclusion, lines 407-409). This conclusion rests on the finding that a linear model on accumulated GDD (R²=0.53) outperformed most satellite-based models, and that the best satellite configuration (RF with S1+GDD, R²=0.58) provided only a 5.9% RMSE improvement that was not reproduced with XGBoost.

**However, this conclusion may be an artifact of four methodological choices that systematically favor the thermal time baseline over satellite predictors:**

1. **Within-season sampling design**: The study samples 5-6 phenological stages per site-season (Figure 2), from sowing (AGB=0) to ripening. Because GDD monotonically increases through the season and AGB accumulates with phenological progress, the within-season sampling design inflates GDD's apparent predictive power. The model is effectively learning "later samples have higher GDD and higher AGB," which is a tautological temporal relationship, not a demonstration that GDD is sufficient for spatial (field-to-field) transferability at a given growth stage. Satellite data's poor performance may reflect its inability to track this simple temporal progression (NDVI saturates, then declines during senescence), not a failure to detect crop condition differences.

2. **Minimal satellite preprocessing**: Sentinel-1 data were used "without speckle filtering or incidence-angle normalization" (Discussion §4.4), which are standard preprocessing steps for agricultural SAR. PlanetScope data include a sensor transition (Dove to SuperDove) with known radiometric differences. These preprocessing choices may have handicapped satellite predictors by adding noise that obscures the crop condition signal.

3. **Linear baseline vs. nonlinear satellite models**: The GDD baseline uses ordinary least squares (LM), while satellite models use random forest and XGBoost. This conflates two sources of improvement: (1) the value of satellite predictors, and (2) the value of nonlinear modeling. If RF on GDD alone substantially outperforms linear GDD, then the satellite contribution is smaller than reported.

4. **Unresolved predictor screening**: The TODO at line 218 indicates that the process for screening 53 VIs to 43 candidate predictors is not documented. If AGB correlation was used, this constitutes data leakage that inflates all performance estimates.

**Alternative interpretation**: Instead of concluding that "satellite data adds minimal value," a more defensible claim would be: "Under minimal preprocessing and across-phenology sampling, accumulated GDD explains more variance than satellite predictors. However, with optimized SAR preprocessing, radiometrically consistent optical data, and sampling at fixed phenological stages, satellite data's contribution may be substantially larger."

The current framing risks misleading operational users into abandoning satellite data when the evidence only shows that *minimally preprocessed, multi-stage* satellite data is not useful.

## Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths

### Alternative Explanation 1: Low environmental stress variability
The study's site-seasons are Mediterranean systems with winter rainfall, which suggests low water stress during main growth. The finding that adding precipitation and soil moisture to GDD (weather set W) did not improve performance (RMSE=7.01-7.55 vs. 6.24 t/ha for GDD alone) supports this. If the site-seasons had low stress variability (uniform water and nitrogen availability), then there would be little crop condition variability for satellite data to detect, and thermal time would naturally dominate. The conclusion that "satellite data adds minimal value" may apply specifically to low-stress environments, not to stressed systems where satellite data is typically advocated. The paper does not discuss this possibility or report whether any site-seasons experienced water or nitrogen stress.

### Alternative Explanation 2: Satellite data's value is at fixed phenological stages
The operational use case for satellite AGB estimation is often to differentiate fields *at a specific phenological stage* (e.g., identify under-performing fields at anthesis for remedial action). In this use case, GDD has minimal variance (all fields are at ~same thermal time on a given date), and satellite data would be primary. The current study's within-season sampling design tests a different question: can GDD predict AGB across a range of phenological stages? The answer is yes, but this does not prove that GDD is sufficient for field-to-field comparisons at a given stage.

### Alternative Explanation 3: Sentinel-1's modest gain is algorithm-specific
The best configuration (S1+GDD with RF) achieved a 5.9% RMSE reduction, but this gain disappeared with XGBoost (+4.3%). The paper interprets this as "configuration-dependent," but it could also indicate that RF's default hyperparameters happen to work well with S1 data, while XGBoost's do not. If hyperparameters were tuned for each algorithm-predictor combination, S1's contribution might be stable across algorithms, strengthening (or weakening) the conclusion.

## Missing Stakeholder Perspectives

### Perspective 1: Operational crop monitoring practitioners
The paper frames the comparison as "satellite data vs. thermal time" from a research validation perspective. However, operational crop monitoring systems typically use satellite data *in combination with* weather-based crop models (which include thermal time). The relevant question for practitioners is not "should we use satellite or GDD?" but "does adding satellite observations to a GDD-based model improve forecasts enough to justify the cost?" The paper's predictor sets (S1, S2, PS vs. S1+GDD, S2+GDD, PS+GDD) partly address this, but the operational framing is underdeveloped.

### Perspective 2: Farmers and agronomists who need within-field variability
The study uses one soil moisture sensor profile per field, so SM varies in time but is field-uniform at each date. Similarly, GDD and precipitation are from a single weather station per site (1.03 to 20.89 km from fields). This means the baselines cannot detect within-field variability in water availability or thermal conditions. Satellite data's value may be primarily for within-field spatial variability (e.g., variable-rate irrigation or fertilization), which the current design does not test. The statement that the RMSE is "too coarse for variable-rate fertilization" (Discussion §4.3) does not acknowledge that GDD cannot provide within-field variation at all.

### Perspective 3: Remote sensing researchers who advocate for proper preprocessing
The remote sensing community has developed best practices for SAR preprocessing (speckle filtering, radiometric calibration, incidence angle normalization) based on decades of research. By omitting these steps without justification or sensitivity analysis, the paper risks being dismissed as "testing satellite data incorrectly" rather than "testing whether satellite data adds value." A more balanced framing would acknowledge that the findings apply to minimally preprocessed data and that optimized preprocessing may yield different results.

## Observations (Non-Defects)

### Observation 1: Strong methodological transparency
The paper is unusually transparent about its limitations: the TODO comment at line 218, the acknowledgment of SAR preprocessing omissions, the note that predictor sets vary across folds, the disclosure about analyzing the dataset in earlier work with different choices, and the extensive limitations paragraph (Discussion §4.4). This transparency is commendable and allows readers to assess the scope of findings.

### Observation 2: Phenological baseline comparison is a valuable contribution
The explicit comparison against DAS and GDD baselines under the same LOSO-CV scheme is rarely done in remote sensing literature. The recommendation that "AGB studies should report phenological baselines evaluated under the same validation scheme" (Conclusion, line 410) is actionable and could shift methodological norms. This contribution stands regardless of whether the satellite preprocessing issues are resolved.

### Observation 3: Per-fold reporting enables transparency
The decision to report per-fold results in Supplementary Table S2 and to show per-fold ranges in Figure 7 (permutation importance) is excellent for transparency. With only four folds, this level of detail is essential for readers to assess variability and avoid overinterpreting mean values.

### Observation 4: ALE profiles effectively support the "season progress" interpretation
Figure 8's ALE profiles for the most important predictors are all monotonic or nearly so, which strongly supports the interpretation that cumulative optical indices "behaved as measures of season progress." This finding is physiologically plausible and well-visualized.

## Summary
The paper's central claim that "satellite data adds minimal value beyond thermal time" is potentially an artifact of four methodological choices: (1) within-season sampling that favors monotonic GDD over complex satellite relationships, (2) minimal satellite preprocessing (no SAR speckle filtering, PlanetScope sensor transition), (3) comparison of linear baselines against nonlinear satellite models, and (4) unresolved predictor screening. The most vulnerable point is the lack of justification or sensitivity analysis for SAR preprocessing omissions (C3), combined with the unresolved data leakage risk (C1). The strongest counter-argument is that the conclusion may apply only to minimally preprocessed, multi-stage satellite data, not to optimized preprocessing at fixed phenological stages. The paper would be strengthened by resolving the TODO (C1), testing SAR preprocessing impact (C3), discussing the within-season sampling bias (C2), and framing conclusions as specific to the tested conditions rather than general to all satellite AGB estimation.
