# Revision Reviewer3 round2

1. Invalid target variable for harvest forecasting

The secondary objective of the study evaluates the feasibility of forecasting harvest AGB one to four months in advance. However, the response variable used to train and evaluate these forecasting models is the spatial prediction generated by the Stage 1 model, rather than actual in-situ ground-truth measurements. Training a predictive model to forecast the output of another model using lagged variables only measures the internal consistency between the lagged predictors and the Stage 1 model's logic; it provides no empirical evidence regarding the ability to forecast actual, real-world crop biomass. This circular operationalization fundamentally undermines all conclusions related to the harvest forecasting objective.

2. Data leakage in hyperparameter tuning

The study's cross-validation strategy suffers from data leakage, invalidating the reported performance metrics for the in-season estimation models (e.g., the primary result of R²=0.78). The methodology explicitly states that hyperparameter candidates were evaluated on the held-out site-season, and the configuration with the highest mean R² across these test folds was selected and reported as the final performance estimate. Using the test set to select hyperparameters means the model evaluation is no longer independent of the training and tuning process, leading to over-optimistic estimates of spatial transferability. To properly estimate generalization error, hyperparameter tuning must be strictly confined to the training data (e.g., via nested cross-validation or an inner validation split), completely independent of the final held-out test fold.

3. Confounded spatial cross-validation

The leave-one-site-out cross-validation (LOSO-CV) is presented as a rigorous test of spatial transferability. However, because the dataset contains only four site-seasons and perfectly confounds wheat habit (spring vs. winter) with one of the locations, the LOSO-CV fold holding out Villa Baviera is simultaneously testing transferability across geography, soil type, and crop habit. This extreme confounding makes it impossible to determine whether performance drops are due to spatial overfitting or the model's inability to generalize across fundamentally different phenological development patterns. While the authors acknowledge this limitation in the discussion, the structural weakness of using only four highly heterogeneous folds undermines the core claim of establishing a transferable framework. The manuscript would benefit from a more nuanced framing of the LOSO-CV results as a preliminary sensitivity analysis rather than a definitive proof of spatial transferability.

4. Pseudoreplication in prediction modeling dataset

For the Stage 2 prediction models, the authors extract 100 random samples per site from the modeled spatial surfaces, resulting in 400 data points. Because these points are sampled from a smoothed spatial prediction rather than representing independent biological replicates, this approach introduces severe pseudoreplication. The spatial autocorrelation inherent in the Stage 1 prediction maps means these 100 points per site contain highly redundant information, artificially inflating the apparent sample size and potentially leading to overconfident model fits and biased performance metrics during cross-validation.

5. Collinearity confounds permutation importance

The authors correctly note that tree-based models can tolerate collinearity for prediction; however, they subsequently use permutation-based variable importance (DALEX) to interpret the model's driving features. Permutation feature importance is highly sensitive to collinearity, as permuting one correlated feature while keeping others fixed creates unrealistic, out-of-distribution synthetic samples that can severely distort importance scores and partial dependence plots. While the authors acknowledge this collinearity when interpreting the flat importance ranking, the explainability analysis remains methodologically compromised. To ensure the completeness and reliability of the explainable AI framework, the authors should either apply the VIF-reduced feature set prior to the DALEX analysis, or utilize explainability methods specifically designed to handle correlated features, such as conditional permutation importance.

6. Contradictory predictor count and mean rank calculation

Section 2.3.3 states that one of the 43 candidate predictors was removed due to correlation, leaving 42 predictors in the model. However, Section 3.2 claims that the variable ΣPS_GRVI fell to '43rd of 43 variables', which is impossible if only 42 variables were used. Additionally, if the variable ranked 43rd, its minimum possible mean rank across four leads would be 11.5 (assuming ranks 1, 1, 1, and 43), which mathematically contradicts the mean rank of 11.25 reported in the same sentence and in Table 6.

7. Mismatch in Hidango sampling points between text and figure

Section 2.1 states there were 'five [sampling points] in... the first season in Hidango; and six in the second season in Hidango'. However, Figure 1 visually shows 6 sampling points in the northern field and 5 in the southern field, while its caption explicitly identifies the northern field as the first season (2021–2022) and the southern field as the second season (2022–2023). This creates a direct contradiction between the text and the figure regarding which season had 5 versus 6 sampling points.

8. Contradictory sign for Sentinel-1 RMSE change

Section 3.1.1 states that adding Sentinel-1 to the model changed the RMSE by '-0.5% (i.e., a marginal degradation)'. However, a negative change in RMSE represents a reduction in error (an improvement), not a degradation. Furthermore, Table 5 and Section 4.1 correctly report this exact change as '+0.5%', confirming that the '-0.5%' in the text is a contradictory sign error.
